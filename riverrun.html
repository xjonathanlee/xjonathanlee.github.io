River Runner

INFO

In 1968, R. Venturi, Scott Brown, and Izenour brought a Yale studio to the Las Vegas Strip, producing Learning from Las Vegas—a seminal record that reshaped architecture and product design by studying the vernacular on its own terms. This palimpsestic archive applies that same ethnographic lens to equestrian culture. By adopting the candid, unvarnished interview style of the 1968 study, this project strips away tired equine tropes and allows stable masters to speak for themselves.

CONTEXT

Moving past tired “horse people” tropes, this documentary series follows five equestrian trainers to offer an unvarnished, contemporary look at equine management and culture.

The Brief

In order to guide my design decisions and to establish my goal as a designer, I created 3 key principles that best defined my journalistic style, aesthetic and defined how I was to collect footage, these principals also acted as gentle guiding hands whenever I strayed from "the project path".

  • Design Principle One: An Ode to the Canon

    • The aesthetic structure complements the purpose of the film, so letting the grid and the restraint from the design speak to the content.

  • Design Principle Two: Unapologetic

    • Each person like each horse is different, we hold space for honesty, hesitation and self correction to allow for the emotions to come through.

  • Design Principle Three: Grounded

    • Horsemanship and care for the animal comes first always. Always asking "why this work matters"

***As much as I wouldn't like to admit, this project was also about me relearning how to use all my tools I've picked up over the years. Its been a very rewarding experience doing work "a la mano" again.

In absence of a Narrative

We forwent a single cohesive story to personalize each interview to the person in front of the camera—looking at trust and riding as a whole ecosystem rather than sticking to a linear plot.

Dropping an overarching narrative meant treating each conversation as a self-contained exploration. By examining how the head trainers structured their days, we traced how horsemanship operates as a living system of habits and reactions. Unstructuring the story also made room for smaller moments, using a sequence of lead-up questions to let each trainer’s “why” surface naturally through reflections on risk, patience, and off-script moments.
